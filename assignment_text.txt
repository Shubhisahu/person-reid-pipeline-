 
 
 
Computer Vision Internship Assignment: Multi-View 
Person Re-Identification 
1. Context 
Consider a retail store, a campus, or a stadium covered by several cameras with no overlap in 
their fields of view. A person walks past camera A and, a minute later, appears in front of 
camera C. The system needs to recognise that these two sightings are the same individual, 
using full-body appearance rather than face recognition. This is the multi-camera (or multi-view) 
person re-identification problem, usually shortened to ReID. It sits behind retail analytics, 
smart-city, and sports vision systems, and it is close to the kind of work we do at Big Vision. 
 
Your task is to build and demonstrate an end-to-end pipeline for this problem. The pipeline 
should detect people, produce an embedding for each detection, match the same person across 
camera views, visualise the results, and present them in a small dashboard. 
 
We are more interested in how you approach the problem than in a state-of-the-art leaderboard 
score. We want to see how you scope the task, choose your tools, train your models, evaluate 
your results carefully, and explain your reasoning. 
2. What we're evaluating 
This assignment helps us understand your ability across four areas. Each stage of the task 
exercises one or more of them. 
 
1.ΓÇï Computer vision understanding. Detection versus ReID, embeddings and metric 
learning, the multi-camera matching problem, and how ReID is evaluated (query and 
gallery sets, the cross-camera protocol, mAP and CMC). 
2.ΓÇï Model training. Training and fine-tuning models properly: loss design, samplers, 
augmentation, avoiding data leakage, reading training curves, and running a controlled 
experiment. 
3.ΓÇï OpenCV and classical CV. Handling video and image I/O, cropping, drawing, and 
building clear visual output. 
4.ΓÇï Software engineering and code quality. Whether your pipeline is clean, modular, 
reproducible, and readable. 
 
We also pay close attention to documentation and communication. Your notebook should read 
as a clear account of the problem and how you solved it, not a wall of code.

3. The task 
The task has core stages, which are all required, and a stretch section, from which you should 
pick at least one item. The stretch section is deliberately open-ended and is where you can 
show more depth. 
Stage 0: Choose and understand your dataset(s) 
-ΓÇï
Select a public dataset, or datasets, for multi-camera person ReID, and explain in writing 
why you chose it. 
-ΓÇï
Show that you understand the dataset by documenting the number of cameras and 
views, the number of identities, the train, query, and gallery split, and the standard 
evaluation protocol. Explain why ReID uses a query and gallery setup, why matching is 
done across cameras, and why same-camera same-identity matches are excluded when 
scoring. 
-ΓÇï
Licensing and ethics: check the licence, consent basis, and provenance of any dataset 
you use. Some widely cited ReID datasets have been withdrawn over privacy and 
consent concerns and should not be used, so we want to see that you check this and 
make a documented, responsible choice. A short paragraph on the ethical 
considerations of ReID as a technology is welcome. 
-ΓÇï
A practical note on data: ReID training sets are usually pre-cropped identity images, but 
the end-to-end demo in Stages 3 and 4 needs raw multi-camera frames or video. Explain 
how you handle this. For example, you might use one dataset for ReID training and a 
separate raw multi-camera source for the demo, such as a multi-camera tracking dataset 
or a short clip you record from a few angles. If raw multi-camera footage is hard to 
obtain, you may demonstrate cross-view matching using the dataset's own multi-camera 
gallery, as long as you are explicit about what you did. 
 
Some starting points, none of which are mandatory as long as you justify your choice: 
Market-1501, which has 6 non-overlapping cameras and roughly 1,501 identities and is a clean, 
widely used benchmark; or MSMT17, which has 15 cameras and is larger and considerably 
harder, though its access is gated. For raw multi-camera video, options include multi-camera 
people-tracking datasets or a short multi-angle clip you record yourself.

Stage 1: Person-only detector 
-ΓÇï
Build a detector that finds people only. Start from a modern pretrained detector and 
adapt it to the single person class. You could fine-tune on a person-detection dataset, 
restrict or retrain the head, or take another approach you can justify. 
-ΓÇï
Filtering the person class from a COCO-pretrained model at inference is acceptable as a 
baseline, but we want to see some real training or fine-tuning so that we can judge your 
training skills. Describe what you did and why. 
-ΓÇï
Report detection metrics: mAP@0.5, mAP@0.5:0.95, and precision and recall. Report 
inference speed as FPS together with the hardware you ran on. Include a few qualitative 
detection images. 
-ΓÇï
Example detectors include YOLO11, YOLO26, RT-DETR, or another well-justified 
choice. 
Stage 2: Re-identification model 
-ΓÇï
Train or fine-tune a ReID model that maps a person crop to an embedding vector, so that 
crops of the same identity are close together and crops of different identities are far apart 
across different cameras. 
-ΓÇï
We want to see real training understanding rather than a single black-box call. At a 
minimum, expose and explain your backbone choice, the loss (for example, ID 
cross-entropy combined with triplet loss and hard mining, label smoothing, a BNNeck, or 
a margin-based loss such as ArcFace), the sampler (identity-balanced, or PK, sampling), 
the augmentations, the optimiser and schedule, and how you prevent train and test 
identity leakage. You may use libraries such as torchreid, fast-reid, Ultralytics, or timm 
for scaffolding, but you should be able to explain every component and run at least one 
meaningful experiment. 
-ΓÇï
Report results on the standard protocol: mAP and CMC Rank-1, Rank-5, and Rank-10. 
Include a query-to-gallery ranked-retrieval visualisation, showing a query image with its 
top-k gallery matches and marking which are correct, and include a distance or similarity 
matrix. 
Stage 3: Multi-view matching and visualisation with OpenCV 
-ΓÇï
Connect Stages 1 and 2 on raw multi-camera footage. For each frame from each 
camera, detect people, crop them, produce embeddings, and assign a consistent global 
ID to the same person seen across different camera views. 
-ΓÇï
Use OpenCV for the video and image I/O, the cropping, and the drawing. Draw bounding 
boxes labelled with the global ID, use a consistent colour per identity across all views, 
lay the camera views out side by side or in a grid, and show a gallery strip of the crops 
matched to a chosen identity. 
-ΓÇï
Produce a short annotated output, such as an image montage, a GIF, or a video, that 
clearly shows the same person recognised across views.

Stage 4: Dashboard 
Build a small dashboard that shows the whole pipeline in one place. It should cover four things: 
 
1.ΓÇï Detection results and metrics. 
2.ΓÇï Re-identification results, including mAP, the CMC curve, and Rank-k numbers. 
3.ΓÇï Cross-view matches, so you can pick a person and see all of their appearances across 
the different cameras. 
4.ΓÇï Interactive exploration. For example, select a query and view its ranked matches, or 
adjust the matching threshold and watch how the matches change. 
 
Use whatever you prefer, such as Streamlit, Gradio, Plotly Dash, or ipywidgets inside the 
notebook. If it cannot run inline in the notebook, include screenshots or a GIF along with the 
code needed to launch it. 
Stretch: add complexity 
Pick at least one of the following; more than one is welcome. Choose based on your interests 
and the time you have. One item done well, with careful analysis, is worth more than several 
done superficially. 
 
-ΓÇï
Full multi-camera tracking. Add a tracker such as ByteTrack, BoT-SORT, or SORT to 
produce tracklets within each camera, then link tracklets across cameras using ReID. 
Discuss how you handle ID switches and fragmentation. This is the fuller version of the 
problem. 
-ΓÇï
Re-ranking. Implement k-reciprocal re-ranking and report the gain in mAP and Rank-1 
that it gives you. 
-ΓÇï
Backbone or loss ablation. Compare at least two configurations, for example a 
lightweight CNN embedding against a ViT or CLIP-based embedding, or triplet-only 
against ID plus triplet against ArcFace, and analyse the trade-offs in accuracy, model 
size, and speed. 
-ΓÇï
Cross-domain generalisation. Train on one dataset and evaluate on another without 
fine-tuning. Report the drop in performance and explain why ReID models tend to overfit 
to the specific cameras and appearance distribution of their training set. This is a 
well-known and important failure mode in practice. 
-ΓÇï
Robustness. Evaluate on harder cases such as occlusion and low resolution, and 
discuss the results. 
-ΓÇï
Efficiency and deployment. Measure end-to-end latency and throughput, and discuss 
what it would take to run this at the edge or scale it to many cameras. 
-ΓÇï
Failure analysis. Systematically characterise where matching breaks, for example 
under similar clothing, lighting changes, or pose changes, with concrete examples.

5. Deliverable 
Submit a single, well-documented Jupyter or IPython notebook (a .ipynb file) that presents the 
work from start to finish. It should interleave your reasoning, decisions, trade-offs, code, results, 
and analysis in markdown cells, so that a reader can go through it from top to bottom and 
understand both what you did and why. 
 
Near the top, include a short section on your approach and key decisions. Throughout, explain 
your choices: why this dataset, why this detector, why this loss, what surprised you, and what 
you would do with more time. 
 
Do not embed trained weights or datasets in the notebook. Host weights on Google Drive or 
Hugging Face and link to them, and refer to datasets by name or URL. Include the exact 
commands and configuration needed to reproduce your results. 
 
A GitHub repository is optional but appreciated, as are the demo GIF or video and dashboard 
screenshots, embedded or linked in the notebook. 
6. Tips 
-ΓÇï
Get a thin end-to-end version working first, even on a tiny subset, and then improve it. A 
complete pipeline is better than a perfect detector with no ReID attached. 
-ΓÇï
Report honest numbers. A clear account of what did not work is more valuable than 
inflated results. 
-ΓÇï
Watch the evaluation pitfalls. Do not let training identities leak into the test set, follow the 
standard query and gallery protocol, and exclude same-camera same-identity matches 
when computing ReID metrics. 
-ΓÇï
When something is ambiguous, state your assumption and continue. Reasonable, 
well-documented assumptions are exactly what we want to see.
